## Abstract

Harmonic change has been studied corpus by corpus. Here we measure chord-level harmony on 3,539 expert-annotated pieces spanning Western classical music (1565–1931), jazz (1925–2007) and Anglo-American popular music (1949–2002) with one set of measures: seventh and extended chords, non-diatonic chords, which chords prepare the tonic, root motion and chord entropy. Within classical music, seventh chords rise from 2% to 54% of chords; the authentic cadence peaks at 65% of tonic arrivals in 1750–1850 and then declines, with a change point at 1805 (95% CI 1790–1810) that survives composer, corpus and mode adjustment. Popular music retraces that decline in thirty years. Clustering 136 composers and artists on harmonic profile yields six groups that cut across genre and century. In a space-time frame, harmony tracks time within every repertoire; geography adds nothing within classical music or jazz and only a modest signal among modern artists.

## Introduction

Music changes, and the change leaves traces that can be counted. Over the past fifteen years, corpus studies have turned those traces into histories: of timbre and chord types in the Billboard charts^2,3^, of melodic complexity in chart-topping songs^4^, of tonal complexity in two thousand classical recordings^5^, of the modes that organised Western art music before 1750 and dissolved after 1800^6,58^, and of the widening range of pitch classes and intervals composers used between 1360 and 1940^7,8^. The cultural-evolution framework that unifies this work treats a repertoire as a population of works whose statistical properties drift, are selected and occasionally jump^1,10^.

Three questions remain open in that literature, and they are the questions this study addresses. The first is the **shape of long-run harmonic change**. Within classical music there is broad agreement that something called tonal or harmonic complexity rises with the nineteenth century as its pivot^5,7,8,9^, but the evidence is at the level of pitch-class content, automatically inferred labels or audio features, and the measures that music theory itself uses to describe harmonic style — which chords prepare the tonic, how roots move, how predictable the next chord is — have been reported for a single composer^61^, two dozen composers^11^ or a single century^57^, or — for the ratio of plagal to authentic motion — estimated from audio chord recognition^5^ and automatic analysis^9^, not from expert annotations across the whole span of annotated repertoire. The second is **whether the twentieth century simplified or complicated harmony**. Audio and melodic studies report restriction and simplification in popular music^2,4^ and even in classical and jazz^40^; studies of pitch and chord content find complexity roughly flat^38,39^ or stable^16^; and the two camps locate their turning points in different years (1964, 1983 and 1991^3^ against 1975, 1996 and 2000^4^). The audio and melodic studies cannot separate harmony from timbre, arrangement and loudness, and the symbolic studies that can^15,16,39^ cover one popular corpus over three decades. The third is **whether classical, jazz and popular music share one harmonic system**. Theorists have argued both that an “extended common practice” runs from Renaissance polyphony to Bill Evans^17^ and can be captured by one generative grammar^18,19^, and that rock operates distinct tonal systems in which IV- and bVII-based closure replaces V→I^20–23^. The quantitative comparisons so far are synchronic^24,25^ or compare popular repertoires with one another^16^; no study has put expert chord-level measures for the three repertoires on one time axis (a note-network study spans six genres over four centuries, but with heuristic dates and genre labels and no harmonic labels^40^). The infrastructure for doing so now exists^26^, but it has not been used for a diachronic analysis.

A fourth question sits behind the other three and has barely been asked of harmony at all: **does place matter, once time is accounted for?** Music sociology and geography describe cities as sites of harmonic innovation — bebop New York, Merseybeat Liverpool, rockabilly Memphis, Muscle Shoals soul^32,33^ — and network studies place composers and other creators in geographic space^30,31^, but no study relates chord-level harmony to the city in which the music was made. The distance-matrix designs needed for such a test are standard in comparative musicology^35,36,37^, where they show that geographic proximity predicts musical similarity between societies; whether it predicts similarity between composers who share a city and a decade is unknown. One quantitative result points the way: geographic proximity structured the transmission of drum-break sampling until about 2000 and not afterwards^34^.

We address all four questions with a single pipeline applied to 3,539 pieces from five corpora: the Distant Listening Corpus and the When in Rome meta-corpus (classical, Roman numerals, 1565–1931), the Weimar Jazz Database (jazz solos, 1925–2007), the McGill Billboard corpus (Hot 100 singles, 1958–1991) and the Rock Corpus (1949–2002). Every chord in every corpus is converted to one representation — a root relative to the local tonic, a quality, an extension level, and flags for chromaticism and applied function — from which the same sixteen measures (fourteen of them forming a harmonic profile) are computed for every piece. The measures were chosen to speak to the three theoretical debates: seventh-chord and extension rates and non-diatonic rates for the complexity debate; the shares of tonic arrivals prepared by V, IV and bVII, and the rate at which V actually proceeds to I, for the question of cadential syntax; root-motion classes^48^ for the question of a shared grammar; and chord entropy and rarefied vocabulary for predictability. We then place each composer and artist in a harmonic space, cluster them without regard to genre or date, and place each piece in a space-time frame — longitude, latitude and year — in which Mantel and partial Mantel tests ask whether harmonic distance follows geographic distance after temporal distance is removed.

The contribution is therefore not the question of long-run change, which others have asked^6,7,9^, but the measures, the span and the frame: expert chord-level cadence, root-motion and entropy measures from 1565 to 2000; three repertoires on one axis; and the first test of geography against time for harmony itself. We find a rise and fall of the authentic cadence with a datable turning point, a popular-music trajectory that retraces the classical one in thirty years, six cross-genre harmonic groups among 136 composers and artists, and a geographic signal that is absent within classical music and jazz once time is controlled and modest among modern artists. We also find, and report as a methodological result, that one of the standard measures — the seventh-chord rate — is not comparable across annotation traditions in popular music, and we show which conclusions survive that fact.

## Results

### The data

After conversion to the common representation, merging of repeated chords and exclusion of pieces with fewer than eight chord events, the dataset holds 3,539 pieces and 411,490 chord events: 2,058 classical pieces (1,280 from the Distant Listening Corpus, 778 from When in Rome; 1565–1931), 412 jazz solos (1925–2007), 876 Billboard singles (1958–1991) and 193 Rock Corpus songs (1949–2002). Table 1 gives the corpora; Supplementary Table S1 the per-era counts and the share of minor-mode pieces, which varies from 2% (1950s popular music) to 64% (early Baroque) and 65% (1900–49) and is controlled for below. All rates are means of per-piece values; the 95% intervals are composer-cluster bootstrap intervals (composers resampled with replacement, 400 draws), which are wider than piece-level intervals wherever one composer dominates an era. Eras follow conventional period boundaries for classical music and stylistic periods for jazz and popular music; binning by quarter-century and decade instead (Supplementary Fig. S1) gives the same trajectories.

### Seventh chords go from exception to default

The seventh chord is the clearest monotonic trend in the classical corpus (Fig. 1a). Chords carrying a seventh are 2% of events in the sixteenth century (Goudimel’s 1565 psalm settings contain almost none; Monteverdi and Schütz use them in 6–7%), 15% in the seventeenth, 27% in the late Baroque (Corelli 22%, Couperin 34%, J. S. Bach 27%), 29% in the Classical era, 37% in 1800–49, 43% in 1850–99 (Chopin 48%, Grieg 49%, Tchaikovsky 46%) and 54% in 1900–49 (Medtner 50%, Bartók 69%, Schulhoff 96%). Extensions above the seventh follow later: 1–3% of events before 1800, 3% in the early Romantic era, 7% after 1850 and 18% after 1900.

Jazz begins where late Romanticism ends. Swing-era lead sheets carry sevenths on 63% of chords; by bebop (1945–59) the figure is 91%, and it stays there (90% in 1960–74, 88% after 1975). What continues to change in jazz is the extension layer, which rises from under 1% in the swing era to 12% in the modal and post-bop years — the move classical music made between 1850 and 1930, compressed into twenty years. Jazz chord symbols and classical Roman numerals have not been calibrated against each other on the same music, so the jazz and classical levels of this measure are compared with that caveat (Discussion); the within-repertoire trends do not depend on it.

Popular music runs the other way. On the Billboard chord symbols, sevenths fall from 34% of chords in 1950–64, when doo-wop, rhythm and blues and rock and roll still carried the blues dominant seventh, to 29% in 1965–74, 27% in 1975–84 and 24% after 1985, while extensions rise from 3% to 8%. This result is stated for the Billboard corpus alone, for a reason given under Robustness: the Rock Corpus’s Roman-numeral convention records sevenths on 4.6% of chords in the same recordings that Billboard’s chord symbols record at 23.5%.

### The authentic cadence rises, peaks and declines

The share of all arrivals on the tonic that come from the dominant — the authentic cadence as a surface statistic, not a formal cadence in the sense of Caplin^54^ or Nobile^23^ — traces an arc (Fig. 1b; Table 2). It is 50% in the Renaissance sample, 54% in the early Baroque, 63% in the late Baroque, 65% in the Classical era and 64% in 1800–49, then 52% after 1850 and 43% after 1900. The probability that a V chord goes on to I at all behaves differently before 1750 and similarly after: 70%, 68%, 63%, 66%, 65%, 55%, 51% across the same seven eras, so that the eighteenth century’s cadential stereotypy lies in which chords reach the tonic rather than in what follows V. Tonic arrivals from IV fall from 22% in the Renaissance to 7–10% from 1700 onward and stay there.

A single change point fitted to the piece-level series places the turn in the authentic cadence at **1805** (95% bootstrap CI 1790–1810; ΔBIC against a straight line +260; a second break adds little, ΔBIC +2), and the turn in descending-fifth root motion at 1770 (1750–1810; +192). The arc is not an artefact of which composers happen to be sampled in each era, of which corpus annotated them, or of the shifting share of minor-mode pieces: in a mixed-effects model with a random intercept per composer and fixed effects for corpus and mode, the slope of the authentic-cadence share changes by −0.30 per century after 1850 (95% CI −0.44 to −0.16), the probability that V proceeds to I by −0.22 (−0.36 to −0.09) and the descending-fifth share by −0.14 (−0.22 to −0.06); the mode coefficients are small (−0.03 for V→I share) and leave the hinge terms unchanged (Supplementary Table S5).

Popular music compresses this three-century arc into thirty years. In 1950–64 V still prepares 47% of tonic arrivals and IV 34%; in 1965–74 the two are level (36% and 35%); by 1985 IV→I (28%) and V→I (34%) are joined by bVII→I, which rises from 2% to 14%, and a V chord proceeds to I less than half the time (45%, against 70% in 1950–64). In the cross-repertoire mixed model (year × genre, random intercept per artist) the V→I share falls by 0.33 per century in popular music against 0.02 in classical music over the same years. The most frequent transition in 1950–64 is I→IV, followed by IV→I; by 1985 IV→I leads. Jazz keeps the dominant: V prepares 67% of tonic arrivals in the swing era and 65% in bebop, and V proceeds to I 85% of the time in bebop — higher than in any classical era — before loosening in the modal years (50% and 77%).

### Chromaticism is flat for two centuries, then climbs

Non-diatonic chords — chords whose root or quality does not belong to the local key — are 13% of events in the Renaissance sample, 22% in the early Baroque and then between 20% and 24% from Corelli to Schubert, a plateau of two hundred years (Fig. 1c). They rise to 29% after 1850 and 40% after 1900. Chords on chromatic roots, a stricter measure, are under 1% in the Renaissance, 3% in the Baroque, 6% in the Classical and early Romantic eras, 8% in the late Romantic era and 15% after 1900; the mode-adjusted mixed model gives this measure a slope change of +0.13 per century after 1850 (CI +0.07 to +0.19). Because minor keys carry more non-diatonic chords under our rule (the minor coefficient is +0.09 for the non-diatonic rate), the era means partly reflect the mode mix; the mixed-model estimates do not. Jazz non-diatonic rates (29–38%) match late Romantic and early-twentieth-century classical values. Popular music is the most diatonic music in the corpus, at 10–16%.

### Vocabulary, predictability and root motion

The number of distinct chord types in a random sample of forty chords (rarefied vocabulary) is 9.5 in the Renaissance sample and then almost constant from 1600 to 1900, at 13.6–14.6, before jumping to 20.4 after 1900 (Fig. 1d). The pooled entropy of the next chord given the current one rises from 2.7 bits in the Renaissance to 3.5 in the early Baroque, dips to 3.1 in the Classical era — the most predictable music in the corpus, as a chord-embedding study of part of the same data also found^11^ — and climbs through 3.6 (1800–49) and 4.0 (1850–99) to 4.5 bits after 1900. Jazz is far more predictable than its chord vocabulary suggests (2.3–2.6 bits), because the ii–V–I cycle dominates its transitions (ii7→V7 and V7→I7 are 8% of all bebop transitions each). Popular music lies between (2.5–3.3 bits), rising from the 1950s to 1975–84 and falling back to 3.0 after 1985.

Root motion separates three grammars (Fig. 2). Classical and jazz harmony run on descending fifths: 30–36% of root moves in every classical era from 1600 to 1850, and 42–56% in jazz. Rock after 1965 runs on steps and thirds: step motion rises from 19% of root moves in 1950–64 to 27% in 1965–74 and 33–34% after 1975, while descending fifths fall from 36% to 23%. Classical music after 1850 drifts in the same direction, with descending fifths falling from 35% to 24% by 1900–49 and third motion doubling from 10% to 19%. The most frequent chords tell the same story: I, V7, V, i, IV, ii in the Classical era; I7, V7, ii7, IV7, VI7 in bebop; I, IV, V, i, vi in 1965–74 rock, with V7 — the first or second chord of every classical era from 1750 — fifth.

### Composers and artists on one harmonic map

The 136 composers and artists with at least five located pieces were placed in a fourteen-dimensional harmonic space (the measures above, with tonic share and minor-quality share in place of rarefied vocabulary, which is undefined for short pieces) and clustered by Ward’s method into six groups, without reference to genre or date (Fig. 3). The groups cut across both, and we describe them before the statistics that qualify them:

1. **Bebop to contemporary jazz** (40): from Eldridge and Lester Young through Parker, Coltrane, Rollins and Getz to Lovano, Redman and Brecker, with Marvin Gaye, and Kenny Rogers as a border member.
2. **Chromatic and extended** (8): Bartók and Schulhoff with the post-bop players Dolphy, Shorter, Henderson, Dorham and Liebman, and Stevie Wonder.
3. **Triadic, diatonic, plagal** (24): Goudimel’s 1565 psalm harmonisations with the Beatles, Dylan, the Rolling Stones, the Beach Boys, Otis Redding, Wilson Pickett, the Eagles, Abba, Elton John and Billy Joel.
4. **bVII- and step-driven rock** (11): Led Zeppelin, Bowie, Heart, the Police, Michael Jackson, Tina Turner, the Pet Shop Boys.
5. **Dominant-seventh diatonic** (19): Händel and the Lieder composers Reichardt and Lang with early jazz (Armstrong, Dodds, Bechet, Goodman) and rock and roll and rhythm and blues (Chuck Berry, Elvis Presley, James Brown, Ray Charles, the Everly Brothers).
6. **Common practice** (34): every other classical composer, from Peri, Monteverdi and Corelli to Rachmaninoff, with the band Chicago as the one modern member.

The pairings are the finding that a genre-blind clustering produces: a sixteenth-century psalter and 1960s pop share triads, diatonic roots and a plagal, weak-dominant syntax; Bartók’s bagatelles and Wayne Shorter’s tunes share chromatic roots and extended chords; a 1920s New Orleans band and a 1950s Memphis record share dominant sevenths over I, IV and V. The first two principal components of the profile (35% and 26% of variance) run from triadic-plagal to seventh-chord, fifth-driven harmony and from cadential to extended, chromatic, step-driven harmony. The partition is moderately separated and not sharply so: silhouette is 0.26 at six groups and nearly flat from four to eight (0.23–0.26), so six was chosen for interpretability rather than by a criterion; k-means reproduces it (adjusted Rand index 0.71) and it is stable under bootstrap resampling of composers (mean ARI 0.77, 90% interval 0.61–0.91). Each composer’s membership confidence (the share of resamples in which it stays with its original cluster-mates; `conf` in the deposited composer table) is below 0.5 for five units: Kenny Rogers (0.24), Billy Joel (0.38), Chicago (0.40), Heart (0.46) and Sonny Rollins (0.50). Earlier work had found composer clustering in classical corpora alone^27,28^ and several harmonic grammars within the Billboard corpus^29^; here both appear in one map.

### Geography after time

Each located piece (3,318 of 3,539; 100% of classical, jazz and Rock Corpus pieces and 75% of Billboard singles) was placed at the city where it was made and its year, in a frame that equates one century with 1,000 km. Ward clustering in this frame yields 25 scenes — Leipzig and Köthen 1720–25, Vienna 1772–1827, Paris 1832–1923, New York 1931–75, Nashville and Memphis 1956–80, London 1963–91 and so on — whose harmonic profiles are listed in Supplementary Table S3 and shown in the interactive space-time cube that accompanies this article (Fig. 4).

Mantel tests on the 136 composers and artists (5,000 permutations; Table 3, with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment over the twenty tests) show that harmonic distance tracks temporal distance within every repertoire. Among the 39 classical composers in 23 cities, harmony correlates with time (r = 0.29, p = 0.014, adjusted 0.028) and not with geography (r = 0.01, p = 0.31; partial r given time −0.03). The 49 jazz performers show the same pattern (time r = 0.23, p = 0.003; geography r = −0.13). The 48 popular artists show a significant temporal signal (r = 0.17, p = 0.011) and a weak, non-significant geographic one (r = 0.10, p = 0.14). Pooling jazz and popular artists (n = 97), however, yields a geographic correlation that survives both time and the adjustment (r = 0.18, p = 0.012; partial r given time 0.19, p = 0.008), and it survives a further partial for genre (r = 0.15, p = 0.022): the signal is not simply New York jazz against London and Los Angeles rock, and the within-genre tests, with 48 or 49 units, cannot resolve it. Pooling all 136 units inverts the pattern — geography significant, time not — and the partial correlation of geography given time is 0.19 (p = 0.002); we attribute this to the Atlantic coinciding with the classical–modern divide, an interpretation the test itself cannot confirm. At the piece level inside the classical corpus, a piece’s harmonically nearest neighbour by another composer is a median 48 years away (random partner by another composer: 85 years, 95% null interval 84–88, p < 0.001) and in the same city 9.9% of the time, against 5.2% for a random partner (p < 0.001) but 9.6% (null interval 8.8–10.6%, p = 0.28) for a random partner no further away in time: the same-city excess is entirely accounted for by temporal proximity.

### Robustness

Six checks were run after the main analysis (Methods; Supplementary Tables S4–S9). (1) *Notation.* Twelve recordings transcribed in both the Rock Corpus and McGill Billboard allow a paired comparison of the two annotation traditions. Among the fourteen profile measures, cadence shares, root-motion classes and chromaticism agree closely (r = 0.85–0.98; paired mean differences with 95% intervals that include zero and are smaller than the between-era differences interpreted above, except for IV→I, whose interval of −0.19 to +0.02 is comparable to them). Seventh-chord rate does not agree (4.6% versus 23.5%; 95% CI of the difference +0.03 to +0.35, Wilcoxon p = 0.01), nor do the pooled entropy and vocabulary measures (r = 0.62–0.69). Seventh and extension rates for popular music are therefore reported from Billboard alone and treated as unmeasured for Rock Corpus pieces in the composer clustering; with n = 12 the agreement on the other measures is evidence of comparability, not proof of equivalence. (2) *The two classical corpora.* Six composers appear in both the Distant Listening Corpus and When in Rome; on different pieces, the two corpora give seventh-chord rates within 0.05 of each other for five of the six and chromatic-root rates within 0.03 for five of the six (Supplementary Table S9), while When in Rome gives a higher V→I share for three of the six (Bach 0.65 against 0.53, Mozart 0.78 against 0.65 on ten pieces, Beethoven 0.71 against 0.65), on different works, so the offset mixes repertoire with annotation practice. Because the Renaissance sample is When in Rome only and 1900–49 is mostly DCML, this offset could flatten the two ends of the cadence arc; the corpus fixed effect in the mixed models absorbs it, and the hinge estimates stand. (3) *Composer, corpus and mode effects.* The mixed-effects models keep every main result. (4) *Change points.* A broken line is preferred to a straight line by ΔBIC 24–260 for all seven classical measures tested, but only the authentic cadence (1805, CI 1790–1810), descending fifths (1770, 1750–1810) and V→I resolution (1820, 1810–1860) have a single break located to within a few decades, and only for the authentic cadence does a second break add nothing (ΔBIC +2). For non-diatonic rate, chromatic roots, seventh-chord rate and chord entropy the single break is poorly located (CIs of 100–280 years) and a two-break model is preferred, consistent with gradual or multi-stage rather than punctuated change. (5) *Bars without chords.* Treating a no-chord stretch as a break in the progression rather than bridging it changes no cadence or root-motion share in any era by more than 0.006. (6) *Cluster sensitivity.* Removing any single measure leaves the six groups largely intact (ARI 0.70–0.98); removing all cadence, root-motion or chord-quality measures lowers agreement to 0.63–0.77; average and complete linkage do not reproduce the groups (ARI 0.09 and 0.25), so the groups are a Ward/k-means partition of a continuous space rather than discrete types.
