## Discussion

### One trajectory or three

The measures that bear on the “extended common practice”^17^ give a split answer, and the split is informative. On chord quality the three repertoires lie on one line: jazz begins at the seventh-chord rate where late Romantic music leaves off, and its extension layer repeats, in twenty years, the move classical harmony made between 1850 and 1930. On syntax they do not. Jazz keeps the descending fifth and the authentic cadence — V proceeds to I more reliably in bebop than in any classical era — while popular music after 1965 abandons both for step motion, IV→I and bVII→I, as rock theorists have argued from analysis^20–23,53^ and smaller corpora^13,14^. The composer map puts the point more sharply: the groups that emerge without reference to genre pair a sixteenth-century psalter with 1960s pop, Bartók with Wayne Shorter, and New Orleans jazz with Memphis rock and roll. The result is consistent with the synchronic finding that classical and popular repertoires share a core of progressions and differ in their characteristic idioms^24,25^, and it adds the time axis: the shared core is the diatonic triad and the fifth, and the idioms that separate the repertoires are the ones that changed fastest after 1850 and after 1965.

### Punctuated or gradual

The classical series contain both kinds of change, and the change-point analysis sorts them. The measures of which chords are used — seventh-chord rate, chord entropy — move smoothly: their single break is unlocated and a two-break model fits better, and the seventh-chord slope steepens after 1850 (+0.15 per century, CI +0.03 to +0.26) without a turning point. The measures of how chords follow one another — the authentic cadence, descending-fifth motion, the probability that V reaches I — turn at points that can be dated to within a few decades: 1805 (1790–1810) for the cadence, 1770 (1750–1810) for descending fifths, 1820 (1810–1860) for V→I resolution. Those dates fall where period studies of audio features place a boundary^5^ and where chord-embedding predictability is at its maximum^11^. Broze and Shanahan’s observation for jazz, that chord frequencies drift while transitions jump^12^, therefore generalises to classical music. For popular music the largest change in any measure between adjacent periods is the fall of V→I and rise of IV→I between 1950–64 and 1965–74, which coincides with the first of Mauch et al.’s three revolutions (1964)^3^ and precedes the melodic simplification dated to 1975^4^. After 1975 the cadential measures keep moving in the same direction — bVII→I from 12% in 1975–84 to 14% after 1985, V→I resolution from 55% to 45% — while chord vocabulary and entropy are flat, which agrees with the finding of a stable US-pop chord vocabulary 1958–1991^16^ and with the loop-based, schema-poor harmony reported for the charts after 1991^56^.

### Complexity

Whether twentieth-century music became simpler depends on the measure and the repertoire. Classical music became more complex on every measure we have (sevenths, extensions, chromaticism, vocabulary, entropy), and the acceleration after 1850 is robust to composer, corpus and mode effects; the plateau after 1800 reported for choral music from audio^55^ does not appear in these expert chord labels. Jazz is complex in vocabulary and chord quality and simple in syntax; its transition entropy (2.3–2.6 bits) is the lowest in the corpus, below the Renaissance sample. Popular music is diatonic, triadic and, after 1965, syntactically loose: its transition entropy rises from 2.5 to 3.3 bits while its vocabulary stays small. These are three different profiles, and a single “complexity” axis^2,4,40^ cannot represent them; two of the three principal components of the composer space are needed. The disagreement between audio studies that find restriction^2^ and symbolic studies that find stability^16,38^ is, in our data, a disagreement about which component is being measured.

### Geography

Within classical music and within jazz, harmony follows time and not place: composers who share a city and a decade are no more alike than composers who share only the decade, and the same-city excess among harmonically nearest neighbours disappears against a time-matched null. Two readings are available. One is that harmonic style in these repertoires travelled faster than people did: scores, lead sheets and recordings circulated, and a composer in Bergen in 1890 wrote the harmony of 1890 rather than the harmony of Bergen. The other is that the geographic signal lives at a scale the within-repertoire tests do not reach. Our cities lie mostly in two regions, so those tests ask whether London differs from Vienna or Memphis from Los Angeles, not whether Europe differs from North America; the pooled test, which does ask that, finds geography significant and time not, but cannot separate the Atlantic from the classical–modern divide. The one positive result is in between: among the 97 jazz and popular artists together, geographic distance predicts harmonic distance after time and genre are removed (partial r = 0.15, p = 0.02), a signal that the 48-unit popular test shows in the same direction without reaching significance (r = 0.10). The modern scenes that drive it — Nashville, Memphis and Macon against London and Los Angeles, New York jazz against the West Coast — are the ones that scene sociology describes^32,33^, and the time-dependent weakening of geographic structure found for sampling after 2000^34^ and the regional structure found in contemporary charts^37^ suggest where to look next: at a finer temporal grain, in the decades before recordings circulated freely. Country-level diffusion of music videos^60^ and society-level comparisons of song features^36^ find geographic structure at larger scales than ours; the present result says that, at the scale of cities within one region, harmonic style was not local once its date is known. Geographic distance between cities on different continents, and the sampling of only two regions, also limit the power of the pooled test, which we therefore do not interpret further.

### Limitations

Four limitations bound these claims. First, the corpora annotate differently. The paired test shows that cadence, root-motion and chromaticism measures survive the change from Roman numerals to chord symbols and seventh-chord rate does not; we have handled the measure that fails, and the two classical corpora agree on shared composers, but jazz chord symbols and classical Roman numerals have never been compared on the same music (a jazz “I7” is a notated chord that an analyst of Brahms might fold into a plain I), and national annotation traditions label chromatic chords differently^50^. The within-repertoire trends are the safer findings; the cross-repertoire levels of chord-quality measures are the least safe. Second, sampling is uneven and canonical: 1700–49 is 739 pieces, 561 of them by Bach; 1900–49 is 92 pieces by eleven composers; Billboard is the Hot 100, the Rock Corpus a critics’ canon weighted toward the 1960s, the Weimar database solos selected for transcription. Capping every composer at thirty pieces per era and resampling (Supplementary Fig. S2) moves no era share by more than 0.04 and no entropy by more than 0.06 bits, the largest shifts being in the early Baroque, but the corpora cannot tell us about music that was not canonised; crowd-sourced resources^59^ would extend the popular side at the cost of annotation quality. Third, the mode mix changes over history^51^ and our chromatic-root rule treats some chords as chromatic in minor keys; the mixed models adjust for mode and the hinge estimates are unchanged, but the era means in Table 2 are not mode-stratified. Jazz harmony here is the lead sheet under an improvised solo^47^, not the voicings played, and dates are recording dates. Fourth, the locations are the cities where the music was made, verified against biographical sources for all 412 musician entries (Supplementary Data 1), but at city resolution, and for 15% of modern pieces the source could not settle the city in the relevant year (marked low confidence); the geographic null should be read as a null at that resolution and within those regions. Twenty Mantel tests, ten mixed models and fourteen paired tests were run; we report the Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment for the Mantel family, under which the within-repertoire temporal correlations and the modern geographic one survive, and treat the mixed-model and paired tests as descriptive of effects whose direction was fixed in advance.

### What the result means for the history of harmony

The arc of the authentic cadence is the central finding. The dominant’s hold on the tonic was not a fixed property of tonal music but a statistic that rose from one tonic arrival in two around 1600 to two in three around 1800 and fell back to two in five by 1920 and one in three in the popular music of the 1980s. The mechanisms differed — chromatic substitution and extended tonality in art music, modal and plagal syntax in rock — and on other measures (root motion, entropy) the twentieth century is far from the sixteenth. But on the measure that defines the common practice, the sixteenth century and the twentieth resemble each other more than either resembles the eighteenth. That is the sense in which the common practice was a period.

## Methods

**Corpora.** Five expert-annotated chord corpora were used (Table 1). The *Distant Listening Corpus* (DCML, EPFL) supplies Roman-numeral harmony annotations in the DCML standard^41–43^ for 1,280 movements and songs from Monteverdi (1590) to Schulhoff (1931), with composition dates from the corpus metadata. The *When in Rome* meta-corpus^44^ supplies 778 further Roman-numeral analyses of works not present in DCML (Bach chorales, Goudimel psalm settings, Monteverdi madrigals other than DCML’s, Haydn quartets, the remaining Beethoven sonatas and about 180 Lieder; duplicates were identified by composer and work and the DCML analysis kept), read with music21; dates were assigned per opus or collection and are accurate to about five years. The *Weimar Jazz Database*^46,47^ supplies the lead-sheet chord sequence, key and recording year for 412 transcribed solos (1925–2007); the chords are those of the tune, not the voicings played. The *McGill Billboard* corpus^45^ supplies Harte chord symbols and a tonic for 876 Hot 100 singles (1958–1991), dated by chart entry; the *Rock Corpus*^14^ supplies Roman-numeral analyses of 193 songs from the Rolling Stone 500 (1949–2002), dated by release year. The three modern corpora were taken from the ChoCo integration^26^. Corpora are the units of annotation practice and are entered as a fixed effect in the mixed models.

**Unified chord representation.** Every chord label was converted to the same event: a root expressed as a pitch class relative to the local tonic (the key of the annotation for Roman-numeral corpora; the annotated tonic for Billboard; the lead-sheet key for Weimar, with a mode inferred from the quality of chords on the tonic); a quality (major, minor, diminished, augmented, suspended, power, augmented sixth, other); an extension level (triad, added sixth, seventh, ninth or above); a seventh type; flags for a chromatic root (not in the major scale, or in minor not in the harmonic-minor collection), applied function and inversion; a duration in beats where the source gives one; and a cadence label where the DCML annotations give one. Minor-tonic songs are encoded relative to their own tonic (parallel encoding), the choice de Clercq recommends as the lesser evil for popular music^49^. Consecutive identical labels were merged, since the popular corpora repeat a chord bar by bar; a piece then contributes one event per change of harmony. Pieces with fewer than eight events after merging were dropped (3,539 remain). Spans annotated as having no chord were excluded from chord counts and, in the main analysis, bridged for transition counts; the strict alternative is reported under Robustness.

**Measures.** Sixteen measures were computed per piece; fourteen of them form the harmonic profile used for clustering and distances. *Chord quality:* seventh rate (share of events with a seventh or higher extension), extension rate (ninth or above), non-diatonic rate (root or quality outside the key), chromatic-root rate, minor-quality share and tonic share. *Cadential syntax:* the share of arrivals on a tonic triad that come from scale degree 5 (V→I share), from 4 (IV→I) and from flat 7 (bVII→I), and the probability that a chord on degree 5 is followed by one on degree 1 (V resolves to I). *Root motion:* following Meeùs’s harmonic vectors^48^, each root move was classed as a descending fifth, ascending fifth, step (one or two semitones in either direction), third, tritone or repetition, and the shares of descending fifths, steps and thirds were taken; collapsing chords and moves into a few functional classes is the information-theoretically justified way to compare corpora with different chord vocabularies^52^. *Predictability:* the Shannon entropy of the piece’s chord-label distribution. These fourteen form the profile. Two further measures are reported in the era tables but not used for clustering because they are undefined for short pieces: the expected number of distinct labels in a random sample of 40 events (rarefied vocabulary, 30 draws) and beats per chord where durations are available. All measures are means over pieces within a group, so that long pieces do not dominate; 95% intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over composers (composer-cluster bootstrap, 400 resamples), so that the 561 Bach pieces count as one composer’s evidence; piece-level intervals, which are narrower, are given in the repository. Two pooled measures — the entropy of the next chord given the current chord, and the pooled chord distribution — were computed per era from all transitions in that era.

**Periods.** Classical pieces were grouped into Renaissance (before 1600), early Baroque (1600–99), late Baroque (1700–49), Classical (1750–99), early Romantic (1800–49), late Romantic (1850–99) and early twentieth century (1900–49); jazz into swing (before 1945), bebop and hard bop (1945–59), modal and post-bop (1960–74) and 1975 onward; popular music into 1950–64, 1965–74, 1975–84 and 1985 onward. The same measures were also binned by 25 years (classical) and by decade (jazz and popular), omitting bins with fewer than five pieces.

**Mixed-effects and change-point models.** For each classical measure a linear mixed model was fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (statsmodels 0.15): metric \~ year (per century, centred on 1750) + a hinge term max(0, year − 1850) + corpus + mode (minor = 1), with a random intercept per composer (59 composers, 2,058 pieces); the models without the mode term are also reported. The 1850 hinge is a fixed, pre-specified test of whether the slope changes in the second half of the period, not an estimate of the change year, which the change-point analysis provides. Across repertoires, metric \~ year × genre with a random intercept per composer or artist. Change points were located by least-squares hinge regression on piece-level values over a grid of candidate years (1600–1910, 5-year steps), with a 95% confidence interval from 200 bootstrap resamples of pieces and model comparison by BIC against a straight line and against a two-break line.

**Locations.** Each piece was assigned the city where it was made: for classical composers, the composer’s residence timeline at the piece’s date (J. S. Bach’s 1720 solo works in Köthen, the 1725 chorales in Leipzig; Chopin in Warsaw to 1830 and Paris thereafter); for jazz performers, the recording base of the period; for popular artists, the city in which the records were made (London for the Beatles from 1963, Los Angeles for Motown artists after the label’s 1972 move, Muscle Shoals for Wilson Pickett). Every one of the 412 musician entries (59 classical composers, 74 jazz performers, 279 popular artists) was checked against a cited biographical source (English Wikipedia, national biographical dictionaries, encyclopaedias; Supplementary Data 1 gives the source for each): 269 timelines were confirmed, 72 corrected, and 71 left as assigned because the source did not settle the year in question (marked low confidence). Coordinates come from a gazetteer of 105 cities. 3,318 pieces (94%) were located.

**Space-time frame and scenes.** Each located piece was placed at (x, y, t), with x and y in equirectangular kilometres and t = year × 10 km, so that one century equals 1,000 km and a decade counts as much as 100 km — a scale at which a composer’s working life in one city forms a compact cloud. Ward clustering of these coordinates, cut at 26 clusters with clusters under eight pieces merged into their nearest neighbour within 1,500 km-equivalents, gave 25 scenes, each profiled by the mean of the fourteen measures.

**Composer space and clustering.** The 136 composers and artists with at least five located pieces were described by the mean of the fourteen measures, standardised with piece-level means and standard deviations. For artists represented only in the Rock Corpus, seventh and extension rates were treated as unmeasured (see Robustness) and imputed with the median of the other popular artists. Ward clustering on all fourteen standardised measures was cut at six groups; silhouette scores for two to ten groups, agreement with k-means (adjusted Rand index), and stability under 300 bootstrap resamples of composers were computed, with each composer’s membership confidence defined as the share of resamples in which it was grouped with its original cluster-mates. For display, the first three principal components were computed from the same matrix (a varimax factor solution is offered in the interactive map); grouping and display are separate steps.

**Mantel tests.** For each subset of composers and artists, three distance matrices were formed: harmonic (Euclidean in the standardised fourteen-measure space), temporal (absolute difference of median years) and geographic (great-circle distance between cities). Mantel correlations with 5,000 permutations tested harmony against time and against geography; partial Mantel correlations (residual method) tested each after removing the linear effect of the other, and, for the pooled modern subset, after also removing a same-genre indicator matrix. The twenty p-values of the Mantel family were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. At the piece level within the classical corpus, each piece’s harmonically nearest neighbour by a different composer was compared on year gap and on sharing a city with two nulls: a random partner by a different composer (1,000 draws) and a random partner by a different composer no further away in time than the observed neighbour (300 draws).

**Robustness checks.** (1) Twelve recordings transcribed in both the Rock Corpus and McGill Billboard were matched by title and performer (same recording, covers excluded) and every measure compared by paired Wilcoxon test, Pearson correlation and the 95% interval of the paired mean difference, judged against the range of that measure across the four popular-music periods. (2) Composers present in both classical corpora were compared on their per-corpus means. (3) The mixed models above, with and without the mode term. (4) The change-point models above. (5) The whole pipeline was re-run with no-chord spans treated as breaks in the progression, so that no transition is counted across them. (6) The six-group solution was recomputed with each measure removed in turn, with each family of measures removed, with k-means and with average and complete linkage, and with composer profiles from the strict no-chord run, and compared with the published solution by adjusted Rand index.
