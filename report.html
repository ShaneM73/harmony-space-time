Summary

Across 3,539 analysed pieces and 411,490 chord events from 1565 to 2007, Western harmony shows one long, measurable drift: chords get richer, the key gets looser, and the dominant stops being the only way home.

  • Seventh chords rise from 2% of chord events in the Renaissance to 27% in the late Baroque, 43% in the late Romantic era and 54% in early-20th-century classical music; jazz pushes them to 91% by bebop. Rock and pop stay near 25%, closer to Mozart than to Monk.

  • The authentic cadence peaks around 1750–1850: 65% of all arrivals on the tonic come from V in the Classical era, then 52% after 1850 and 43% after 1900. Rock follows the same curve in 30 years: 47% in 1950–64, 34% after 1985, as IV→I (28–35%) and bVII→I (2% → 14%) take over.

  • Chromaticism is flat for 200 years and then climbs: non-diatonic chords are 20–24% of events from Corelli to Schubert, 29% in the late Romantic era and 40% after 1900. Rock harmony is the most diatonic music in the corpus (10–16%).

  • Vocabulary barely changes from 1600 to 1900 (about 14 distinct chord types per 40 chords) and then jumps to 20 after 1900; transition entropy, how unpredictable the next chord is, rises steadily from 2.7 bits (Renaissance) to 4.5 bits (1900–49).

  • Three grammars: classical and jazz harmony run on descending fifths (30–36% and 42–56% of root moves); rock after 1965 runs on steps and thirds (step motion 27–34%), with I–IV–V and bVII as its core.

The interactive version of every chart is in the Harmony Across Four Centuries dashboard.

Method

Every chord label, whatever its source notation, was reduced to the same six features so that a Bach chorale, a Coltrane change and a Billboard single can be compared on one scale.

  1. Root as a scale degree: semitones above the local tonic (0–11). DCML gives the root relative to the local key directly; When-in-Rome labels were parsed with music21; Rock Corpus files carry it; Billboard and Weimar chord roots were subtracted from the annotated tonic.

  2. Quality: major, minor, diminished, augmented, suspended, power chord, augmented sixth.

  3. Extension: triad, seventh, or ninth and above (including added ninths, elevenths, thirteenths and altered dominants).

  4. Non-diatonic: the root is outside the local key, or the quality is not the one the key supplies (a major II, a minor IV, a major bVI). In minor, the raised leading tone and both forms of V count as diatonic. This one rule catches secondary dominants, modal mixture, Neapolitans and augmented sixths in every corpus; the stricter “chromatic root” count is reported alongside it.

  5. Applied chord and inversion: taken from the Roman-numeral corpora only (DCML, When in Rome, Rock Corpus); Billboard slash chords were read as inversions.

  6. Duration in beats: notated quarter-note beats for classical, bar positions for the modern transcriptions.

Consecutive identical chords within a piece were merged, which matters for pop transcriptions that write one symbol per bar (Billboard shrank from 112k to 84k events). Mode was taken from the annotated global key, or for Billboard and Weimar inferred from the quality of chords built on the tonic.

Every rate is computed per piece first and then averaged over pieces, so long works do not dominate. Intervals are the 2.5th–97.5th percentiles of 400 bootstrap resamples of pieces. Transitions are counted within pieces only. Era bins are 25-year composition-date windows for classical music, decades for jazz and rock/pop; named eras are coarser groupings of the same bins. Transition entropy is H(next chord | current chord) over Roman-numeral-plus-extension labels, pooled over the era. Vocabulary is the expected number of distinct labels in a random sample of 40 chords from a piece (rarefaction, so long pieces are not favoured).

Code: load_dcml.py, load_wir.py, load_modern.py build the unified table; analyze.py computes everything above; export_dash.py writes the dashboard tables.

Finding 1: seventh chords go from exception to default

ClassicalJazzRock & pop

Share of chord events with a seventh or higher extension; 95% bootstrap bands. Computed from the five corpora; bins with fewer than 3 pieces omitted.

The classical line rises almost monotonically for 350 years. Goudimel's 1565 psalm settings contain virtually no sevenths; Monteverdi and Schütz use them in 5–8% of chords; Corelli's trio sonatas (1680s) jump to 22%, and from Bach to Beethoven the figure sits at 25–34%. The Romantic generation (Chopin 48%, Grieg 49%, Tchaikovsky 46%) pushes it past 40%, and the early 20th century (Medtner 50%, Bartók 69%, Schulhoff 96%) past 50%. Extensions above the seventh follow later and faster: under 2% until 1800, 3% in the early Romantic era, 7% after 1850 and 18% after 1900.

Jazz starts where late Romanticism ends. Swing-era lead sheets already carry sevenths on 63% of chords; by bebop (1945–59) it is 91%, after which the figure plateaus. What keeps changing in jazz is the extension layer: ninths and above rise from under 1% in the swing era to 12% in the modal and post-bop years, the same move classical music made between 1850 and 1930.

Rock and pop go the other way. Sevenths sit at 21–29% in every decade from the 1950s to the 1990s, with the 1970s as the high point (soul and funk singles), and extended chords stay under 8%. On this measure a Billboard single of 1985 is closer to a Mozart sonata than to anything recorded by a jazz quintet the same year.

Finding 2: the dominant loses its grip

ClassicalJazzRock & pop

Of all moves onto a tonic chord, the share that come from V; 95% bootstrap bands. Computed from the five corpora; bins with fewer than 3 pieces omitted.

The authentic cadence is a Baroque-to-Classical achievement, not a constant. In the Renaissance and early Baroque, half of all tonic arrivals come from V and a fifth come from IV (plagal and modal motion). By Corelli (1680s) V supplies 64% of them, and from Bach to Beethoven the figure holds at 62–67%, with IV down to 7–9%. After 1850 it slides: 54% in 1850–74, 51% in 1875–99, 42% after 1900. The complementary question, whether a major V chord goes on to I at all, tells the same story: 63–66% of the time from 1700 to 1850, 55% in the late Romantic era, 51% after 1900.

Jazz keeps the dominant longer and more strictly than any classical era: in bebop a V7 resolves to I 85% of the time, because ii–V–I is the unit of the language. The modal turn after 1960 cuts V→I to 50% of tonic arrivals and lets bVII→I and IV→I grow.

Rock compresses three centuries of classical drift into thirty years. In 1950–64 (early rock and roll, doo-wop, Motown) V still prepares nearly half of tonic arrivals and IV a third. From 1965 the two are equal, and by the 1980s IV→I is the commonest way home, bVII→I has gone from 2% to 14%, and a V chord goes on to I less than half the time. The “dominant” has become one option among I, IV, V and bVII rather than the hinge of the key.

Finding 3: chromaticism is flat for two centuries, then climbs

The share of non-diatonic chords (root outside the key, or the wrong quality for its degree) barely moves between 1680 and 1850: 20% in the late Baroque, 22% in the Classical era, 24% in the early Romantic era. The chromatic harmony we associate with Wagner and Liszt shows up as a step rather than a slope: 29% after 1850, 40% after 1900. The stricter count, chords whose root itself is foreign to the key, tells the same story at a smaller scale: 3% from 1600 to 1750, 6% from 1750 to 1850, 8% in the late Romantic era, 15% after 1900.

The Renaissance figure (13% non-diatonic but only 1% chromatic roots) is modal harmony: Goudimel's psalms sit on diatonic roots but freely use major triads where the later system expects minor ones. The early Baroque jump to 22% is Monteverdi and Schütz, whose expressive chromaticism the Baroque then disciplines back to 20%.

Jazz inherits the late-Romantic level (29–32%) and extends it (38% in post-bop), chiefly through tritone substitutions and secondary dominants, which appear here as chromatic roots on bII, bIII, bVI and bVII. Rock is the most diatonic repertoire in the corpus: a 1950s single is 90% diatonic. Its one real chromatic move, the bVII and bVI borrowed from the parallel minor, doubles the chromatic-root rate between 1964 and 1975 (4% to 9%) and then holds.

Finding 4: vocabulary, predictability and root motion

How many different chords a style uses is surprisingly stable: a random 40-chord stretch of Monteverdi, Bach, Mozart or Schubert contains about 14 distinct chord types (root, quality and extension). Only two things move it. The Renaissance sits lower (9.5, Goudimel's psalms use 8), and the 20th century jumps to 20 (Bartók 26, Rachmaninoff 22, Medtner 22). Jazz lead sheets, for all their sevenths, use 11–12 types per 40 chords, because the ii7–V7–I7 cycle repeats through every key; rock uses 7–8.

Predictability moves more smoothly. Transition entropy, the uncertainty about the next chord given the current one, rises from 2.7 bits in the Renaissance to 3.4–3.6 bits across the Baroque and Classical eras, 4.0 after 1850 and 4.5 after 1900. Jazz is the most predictable harmony in the corpus (2.3–2.6 bits: after a ii7 comes a V7), and rock sits between (2.6–3.3 bits).

Root motion is where the three grammars separate most clearly. Common-practice harmony moves by descending fifth 34–36% of the time from 1600 to 1850 (V→I, ii→V, vi→ii, I→IV); jazz raises that to 51–56%, the highest in the corpus, as the circle-of-fifths sequence becomes the default. The 20th century and rock after 1965 both abandon it: fifths fall to 23–24%, and steps (bVII→I, IV→V, I→bVII) and thirds (I→vi, vi→IV, I→bIII) take their place. A 1980s rock song and a Bartók bagatelle share a root-motion profile that neither shares with Bach or Charlie Parker.

Three things stand out.

  1. The Classical era is the most concentrated: V7→I alone is 10% of all progressions, twice its share in any other era, and I, V and V7 together are more than half of all chords. Everything after 1800 is a dispersal from this peak: the top progression falls to 5% in the late Romantic era and 2% after 1900, where even the most common move (V7→i) is rare.

  2. Jazz is built on sevenths and the circle of fifths: every one of its top chords is a seventh chord, and ii7→V7→I7 supplies its two commonest moves. Bebop's top-6 progressions are all descending-fifth moves; by post-bop bII7 (the tritone substitute) enters the top chords and the shares flatten.

  3. Rock is built on I, IV and V as neighbours, not as a hierarchy: I→IV and IV→I are its two commonest moves in every period, V→IV (a “retrogression” in textbook terms) is in the top six after 1965, and bVII is a top-six chord from the late 1960s on. The 1950–64 column, with its V7 and I7, is the blues inheritance that later decades shed.

The dashboard's 12×12 transition matrices (section 7) show the same thing as a picture: classical and jazz moves cluster on the fifth-related diagonals, rock's on I–IV–V and bVII.

  • Couperin, not Bach, is the most cadential composer in the corpus (77% of tonic arrivals from V) and uses more sevenths than Bach (34% vs 27%); the dance-suite idiom is more formulaic than the chorale and fugue.

  • Mozart is the most diatonic composer after 1700 (16% non-diatonic) and among the most cadential (67%); Haydn and C. P. E. Bach, often grouped with him, are nearly twice as chromatic (27–29%).

  • Mendelssohn is the outlier of the Romantic generation: the widest vocabulary before 1900 (19.6 types per 40 chords) and the weakest dominant (49%), closer to Grieg than to his contemporary Chopin, who keeps a Classical cadence rate (63%) under a Romantic seventh-chord rate (48%).

Schulhoff's 1931 Suite dansante en jazz (96% sevenths) is, fittingly, where the classical corpus meets the jazz one.

A space-time frame: where and when each work was made, and whether place matters

Harmony is organised by time in every genre and by place in none of them once genre is held fixed, with one weak exception in rock and pop. That is the result of placing 3,318 located pieces at the city their composer or artist was based in that year, clustering them into 25 space-time scenes, and testing whether composers who are close in harmony are close in years, in kilometres, or both.

How works were located. None of the corpora records where a piece was made, so location is assigned from biography: for the 59 classical composers a residence timeline (Bach's chorales of 1725 in Leipzig, his 1720 solo works in Köthen; Beethoven in Bonn to 1792 and Vienna after; Chopin in Warsaw to 1830 and Paris after; Medtner in Moscow, Berlin and Paris in turn), marked high confidence; for the 74 jazz performers the recording base of the period (New York for most, Los Angeles for the West Coast players, Chicago in the 1920s, Copenhagen for Dexter Gordon and Ben Webster in the 1960s), medium; for the Rock Corpus and the Billboard artists with two or more songs the band's home or base city, medium and low. Coverage is 100% of classical, jazz and Rock Corpus pieces and 75% of Billboard singles. Each piece is then a point at (longitude, latitude, year), with one century scaled to 1,000 km so that a decade counts as much as 100 km.

The scene table already shows the answer. The harmonic groups follow the rows in time order, not in map order: group 4 is every classical scene from Mantua to Vienna (1592–1827), group 3 every classical scene after 1829 plus bebop New York, and the rock scenes split into a Southern US group (Memphis, Nashville, New Orleans) that keeps a dominant-seventh, blues-based idiom, and a London, Los Angeles, Detroit, New York group with weaker dominants. Two cities with the same years sit together whatever their distance (Bergen and Moscow in the 1880s; Vienna and London in the 1780s), while the same city in different periods sits apart (Vienna 1772–1827 against Vienna/Prague 1873–1931; New York 1931–64 against New York 1968–96).

Composer groups. Clustering the 136 composers and artists with five or more located pieces on their mean harmonic profile (Ward, six clusters) gives groups that cut across genre and century:

  1. Bebop to contemporary jazz (38): Eldridge and Lester Young through Parker, Coltrane, Rollins, Getz, to Lovano, Redman and Brecker.

  2. Chromatic and extended (8): Bartók and Schulhoff with the post-bop players Dolphy, Shorter, Henderson, Dorham and Liebman, and Stevie Wonder.

  3. Triadic, diatonic, plagal (26): the four pre-tonal composers (Goudimel, Monteverdi, Frescobaldi, Schütz) with the Beatles, Dylan, the Rolling Stones, the Beach Boys, Otis Redding, the Eagles, Abba, Elton John, Billy Joel and Cyndi Lauper.

  4. bVII and step-driven rock (11): Led Zeppelin, Bowie, Heart, the Police, Michael Jackson, Tina Turner, Pet Shop Boys.

  5. Dominant-seventh diatonic (22): Händel and the Lieder composers Reichardt and Lang with early jazz (Armstrong, Dodds, Bechet, Goodman) and rock and roll and R&B (Chuck Berry, Elvis, James Brown, Ray Charles, the Everly Brothers, Marvin Gaye).

  6. Common practice (31): every classical composer from Peri and Corelli to Rachmaninoff, with the band Chicago as the one modern member.

The pairings are the finding: pre-tonal polyphony and 1960s pop share triads, diatonic roots and a plagal, weak-dominant syntax; Bartók's bagatelles and Wayne Shorter's tunes share chromatic roots and extended chords; a 1920s New Orleans band and a 1950s Memphis record share dominant sevenths over I, IV and V. The first two principal components of the profile (36% and 26% of variance) run from triadic-plagal to seventh-chord-fifth-driven harmony, and from cadential to extended-chromatic-step-driven harmony; the dashboard's composer map (section 10) places every composer and artist on them.

Within classical music, composers close in time are close in harmony (r = 0.29) and composers close in space are not (r = 0.01); the same holds for jazz. Rock and pop show a weak geographic signal that survives controlling for time (r = 0.15, p = 0.06): the Memphis–Nashville–Macon axis against London and Los Angeles. The pooled rows invert this, with geography significant and time not, but that is the Atlantic standing in for the classical–modern divide, and the U-shaped history in which 1600 and 1965 resemble each other more than either resembles 1800. At the piece level inside the classical corpus, a piece's harmonically nearest neighbour by another composer is a median 48 years away (random pairs: 84 years) and in the same city only 10% of the time (random: 14%).

Two cautions. Locations are residences, not places of composition, and for pop artists a home city is a proxy for a scene they may have left; the test is only as good as that assignment. And the classical corpus is thin in the places where a geographic effect would be expected to show (Italy and France after 1750, Russia, England), so "no geographic effect" means "none detectable among the German-Austrian-French-Nordic composers sampled here".

Has this been done? Not in this form. The closest pieces of work:

  • Schich et al. (2014), Science, built the general space-time framework for cultural history from the birth and death places of 150,000 notable people, but with no content features: it maps where culture happened, not what it sounded like.

  • White (2017), Journal of Creative Music Systems, is the nearest in spirit: similarity metrics over corpora of 19 common-practice composers, concluding that "geography and chronology determine much inter-corpus similarity". It does not separate the two or test them against each other, and it stays inside the common-practice era.

  • Georges & Nguyen (2019), Scientometrics, map and cluster 500 classical composers, finding period first and nationality second, but from influence and metadata (period, place, school) rather than from the music, so the geography is built into the input.

  • Weiß, Mauch, Dixon & Müller (2018) cluster 2,000 recordings by tonal features and recover historical periods; no geography.

  • Gleiser & Danon (2003), Advances in Complex Systems, find community structure in the 1912–1940 jazz collaboration network that follows city (Chicago versus New York) and race; collaboration, not harmony.

  • Lee & Cunningham (2012) trace the geographic flow of music taste between 200 cities from listening data; consumption, not composition.

What is new here is the combination: works rather than people as the points, harmonic content as the features, a time scale and a space scale set against each other, and a formal test (partial Mantel) of which one harmony follows, run across classical, jazz and rock on the same features. The result that harmony follows time and not place in classical music and jazz, and weakly follows place in rock, is, as far as this search found, not previously reported.

Related work and what this study adds

Quantitative work on harmonic change exists for each repertoire separately; this study is, as far as the search below found, the first to put human-annotated chord labels from classical, jazz and rock/pop on one feature scale across four centuries. Where the findings overlap with earlier work they agree, which is reassuring; several are not in the literature.

What this study adds:

  1. One scale across genres. Earlier studies measure one repertoire with features chosen for it (harmonic topics from audio for pop, pitch-class profiles for classical, chord quality for jazz). Here the same six chord features and the same per-piece metrics are computed from human annotations in all three, so a statement like “a 1985 single is closer to Mozart than to a 1985 jazz solo in seventh-chord use” can be made on numbers.

  2. The authentic cadence as a plateau, not a constant. Dominant preparation of the tonic is 50% in the Renaissance, 62–67% from 1700 to 1850, and 43% after 1900. The literature describes the rise of functional tonality and its later dissolution separately; measuring the same quantity across both gives the dates (c. 1680 and c. 1850) and the size of the plateau.

  3. Rock recapitulates the classical decline in 30 years. V's share of tonic arrivals falls from 47% to 34% between 1950–64 and 1985+, the same drop classical music took from 1850 to 1930. Mauch and Gauvin saw the chord-frequency side (fewer dominant sevenths, more bVII); the syntactic measure (what precedes the tonic) is new.

  4. Richness and predictability come apart. Chord vocabulary per 40 chords is flat from 1600 to 1900 while transition entropy rises by a full bit; a style can become harder to predict without using more chords. Jazz is the extreme case: the most sevenths and the lowest entropy in the corpus.

  5. A shared root-motion profile for 20th-century classical music and post-1965 rock. Both drop descending fifths to 23–24% and raise steps to 30–34%, which neither shares with Bach (36% fifths) or bebop (56%). Broze and Shanahan found the jazz side of this; the convergence of the other two repertoires has not been reported.

  6. Composer-level results from expert labels: Couperin as the most cadential composer, Mozart as the most diatonic after 1700, Mendelssohn as the Romantic outlier in vocabulary, Bartók's 1908 Bagatelles as the clean break on every measure. Park et al. and González-Espinoza & Plotkin derive composer rankings from scores; this is the first set derived from Roman-numeral annotations across 39 composers.

  7. Methodological: per-piece averaging with bootstrap intervals, rarefied vocabulary, a single non-diatonic rule that works across Roman numerals and chord symbols, and merging of repeated chords so that bar-by-bar pop transcriptions are comparable with harmony-change-only classical labels. The pipeline is open and reruns from the public repositories.

The search was web-based (October 2026) and covered corpus musicology, music information retrieval and computational-musicology venues; a formal literature review would also check the DCML, Yale and Ohio State groups' recent conference papers (ISMIR, SMT, ICMPC) and the Transactions of the ISMIR corpus papers.

Limitations and next steps

The trends above are robust to the obvious checks (per-piece averaging, bootstrap intervals, merging of repeats), but five things limit what they can claim.

  1. The corpora annotate differently. Classical and Rock Corpus labels are expert Roman numerals that already decide what counts as a chord; Billboard and Weimar are chord symbols at the granularity a guitarist or a lead sheet uses. A jazz “I7” is a notated maj7 or 6 chord that an analyst of Brahms might fold into a plain I. Cross-genre comparisons of seventh-chord rate are therefore partly comparisons of notation; the within-genre trends are the safer finding.

  2. Sampling is uneven and not random. 1700–49 is 497 pieces, 371 of them Bach chorales; 1650–74 has two pieces and is omitted; 1900–49 is 92 pieces from seven composers; the 1990s pop bin is 56 Rock Corpus songs and the 2000s is one. Billboard covers Hot 100 singles only, the Rock Corpus a critics' canon, the Weimar database solos chosen for transcription. Each corpus has its own canon bias.

  3. Dates are approximate for When-in-Rome pieces (assigned per opus or collection, within about five years) and for Weimar (recording, not composition: a 1960 recording of a 1935 standard is dated 1960).

  4. Key and mode are the annotators'. For Billboard and Weimar the mode was inferred from the tonic chord's quality, which misreads blues and modal songs; non-diatonic rates there are a little high by construction.

  5. Cadence metrics are local. “V→I” counts any adjacent pair, including passing or inverted dominants, not only phrase-ending cadences. DCML's hand-labelled cadences (PAC, IAC, HC) agree with the trend (2.8 PACs per 100 chords in the Classical era, 1.6 after 1900) but exist for that corpus only.

Next steps that the pipeline already supports:

  • Add the remaining ChoCo partitions (iReal Pro's 2,000+ jazz standards by composer, Wikifonia lead sheets, Isophonics Beatles/Queen) and the Chordonomicon dataset (660k crowd-sourced pop progressions with decade and genre) to extend the modern side to 2020 and break pop out by genre.

  • Fit a per-piece regression (metric ~ year + composer + mode + corpus) to separate period effects from composer and corpus effects; the composer table suggests composer explains more variance than decade within 1700–1850.

  • Compare pitch-class-set measures (chord dissonance, voice-leading distance) from the scores themselves, which the DCML and When-in-Rome repositories carry as MusicXML, to test whether the label-based rise in chromaticism matches the sounding dissonance.

  • Run the same features on key-finding output for audio-derived chord transcriptions (e.g. from the Billboard audio), which would allow the Hot 100 after 1991.
